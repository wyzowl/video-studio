# The Story standard

The review's **Story** score is judged against this page: one clear message, a hook whose promise is paid off, and
one clear ask at the end. The brief and script are written to it, the storyboard keeps it, and the review scores
against the same words. The opening has its own standard (`hooks.md`); the words themselves are in `narration.md`.

## Before writing: know the brief

A vague grasp of the subject makes generic pictures and woolly words, so get specific first. Before a script, know:
1. **What it is and what it really does:** the mechanism, step by step, not just the marketing line. How does a
   person actually use it, and what happens?
2. **Who it's for,** which sets the language, the tone and how much can be assumed.
3. **The one message** the viewer must take away.
4. **The ask:** what the viewer should do at the end (try it, book a call, visit a page, download it).
5. **The length,** and anything that must be in it (figures, features, a line the brand always uses).
6. **The tone:** playful, warm, slick, premium, irreverent.

When any of these is unclear, ask rather than guess: a wrong idea of what the product does sinks the whole film.

## What good looks like

- **One clear message,** stated early and supported by everything after it. A viewer asked what the film said could
  answer in a sentence, and it would be the brief's message.
- **A promise kept.** The opening sets up a problem, a question or a desire, and the film pays it off: the closing
  picture resolves the opening (the queue that opened the film is empty; the cluttered desk is clear; the question on
  screen has its answer), so the film feels like one piece.
- **Benefit-led:** what the viewer gets, not a list of features. "Your invoices chase themselves" beats "automated
  reminder workflows."
- **The product shown where it matters:** at the "how it works" beats, the real interface or product doing the job,
  one benefit a beat.
- **Abstract ideas made concrete:** a metaphor or analogy the picture can carry (a snowball for compounding, a
  funnel for filtering, a relay baton for a hand-over), especially before showing something complex.
- **Flowing, not a slideshow:** each beat picks up from the last and hands on to the next (`beats.md`), and the voice
  carries sentences across beats so the picture can change while the words run on.
- **One clear ask at the end,** with the brand: a memorable line, then exactly what to do and where.
- **The right length:** the words fit the time (`../numbers.md`, "Speech"), with room to breathe.

## How to get there

1. **Find the spine:** the audience, their problem or desire, the promise (one line), the product's role in it, the
   proof, and the ask. Build the order around the promise, not around the brief's or the website's sections.
2. **Choose a shape.** Most films follow a version of this, used as a default and changed when the brief needs
   another shape (a story, a comparison, one big idea):
   1. **Hook:** a relatable problem, a bold statement, an intriguing question, a character (`hooks.md`).
   2. **Stakes** (optional): what it costs to leave the problem alone.
   3. **The answer arrives:** the product or idea is named; the brand appears.
   4. **How it works:** the product in action, one benefit a beat.
   5. **The better world:** the payoff, mirroring the opening.
   6. **The ask:** the brand's line, the logo and the action.
   Each type of video has its own shapes (`types/`).
3. **Pick the visual approach and keep to it:** concept- and interface-led with no characters; characters for the
   human beats and icons and type for the data; full character scenes in real places; one named character's
   journey; abstract characters or a mascot carrying an emotional metaphor; characters as shapes or objects. Choose
   from the company, the type of video and the brief, then keep the world consistent so the beats connect.
4. **Write the rows** (`narration.md`): one or two sentences each, the picture beside them, flowing on.
5. **Check it:** the message stated by the second beat; the ending paying off the opening; one ask; the length right;
   no jargon the audience wouldn't use.

## What lowers it

| What you see | Usual cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| No clear message | several messages competing; the brief's sections in order | one message; reorder and cut to it |
| A feature tour | features listed, never turned into what the viewer gets | each feature as a benefit; cut the ones that don't serve the message |
| The promise forgotten | the opening's problem never comes back | close on the opening resolved |
| Slow to the point | context, history or the company before the value | value by the second beat; the background after, or not at all |
| A vague ending | no ask, several asks, or an ask without where to go | one ask, with the brand and exactly where |
| Too much said | more words than the length allows | cut words, not pauses (`../numbers.md`) |
| Generic | props and claims any company could use | pictures and proof from the source |

## How it's scored

| Score | Means |
|---|---|
| 9–10 | One message anyone would repeat; the opening's promise paid off; every beat earns its place; one clear ask |
| 8 | Clear and well shaped; one beat drifts or the ending could land harder |
| 6–7 | The message is there but diluted: a feature list, a slow start, or an ending that doesn't pay off |
| 4–5 | Several messages, or the order of the source rather than a story |
| 1–3 | No message a viewer could name |

The checks will measure what can be measured in the script (the hook's length, words against the length, the longest
beat: `../numbers.md`); the story itself is judged by reading the script and watching the film.
